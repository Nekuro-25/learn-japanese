## Japanese Writing Systems

Hip hip hooray XD — okay, back to the topic. Japanese uses three main writing systems: **Hiragana**, **Katakana**, and **Kanji**. Together, Hiragana and Katakana are called **Kana**. There's also **Furigana**, but that's not a separate system — it's a reading aid placed above or beside kanji to show pronunciation. I'll explain each one below.

### 1. Hiragana

Let's start with Hiragana, the foundation of Japanese writing. It's used for native Japanese words, grammatical particles, okurigana (verb and adjective endings), and words that aren't usually written in kanji. With its rounded strokes and flowing curves, Hiragana is relatively easy to get used to. There are 46 basic Hiragana characters. Think that's a lot? Nah, bro.

### 2. Katakana

Please don't confuse Katakana with Katana (the Japanese sword) — BUT the two do share a certain vibe. If Hiragana is rounded and flowing, Katakana is all about SHARPNESS. It looks like characters that could slash something. Katakana is mainly used for loanwords, foreign names, onomatopoeia, and emphasis — it's the writing system's way of integrating foreign vocabulary while still respecting Japanese phonology.

### 3. Kanji

I... don't have a lot of opinions about this one. Kanji basically carries all the hard parts of Hiragana and Katakana combined: rounded corners, sharp turns, and — how do I say it? — a lot of strokes on paper. The reason is obvious: kanji originally came from Chinese characters and were adapted for Japanese. Their readings and usage follow Japanese conventions, not Chinese.

### Bonus: Furigana

Furigana isn't a separate writing system — they're small kana characters printed above or beside kanji to show how to pronounce them. You'll see them a lot in manga, children's books, and learning materials.
