We've made it to the third and final Hiragana milestone! By now, you should be familiar with the 46 basic Hiragana characters and the 25 additional forms covered in the first two milestones.

However, recognizing individual characters isn't enough to read Japanese comfortably. We also need to understand how certain kana combine, how consonants are doubled, and how long vowels are pronounced. This milestone covers four important concepts: **Youon**, **Sokuon**, **Chouon**, and **Hatsuon**.

These concepts don't simply add four more basic characters to memorize. They teach us how to interpret kana combinations and pronunciation patterns when reading Japanese.

## 1. Youon (<span lang="ja">拗音</span>) — Combining Kana

Youon is formed by combining a kana ending in the **i-vowel sound** with a small <span lang="ja">ゃ</span>, <span lang="ja">ゅ</span>, or <span lang="ja">ょ</span>. The small kana changes how the combination is read.

For example, <span lang="ja">き</span> + small <span lang="ja">ゃ</span> = <span lang="ja">きゃ</span> (<em>kya</em>), not <em>kiya</em>. The combination is pronounced as one mora rather than two separate morae.

- <strong lang="ja">きゃ / きゅ / きょ</strong> — kya / kyu / kyo
- <strong lang="ja">しゃ / しゅ / しょ</strong> — sha / shu / sho
- <strong lang="ja">ちゃ / ちゅ / ちょ</strong> — cha / chu / cho
- <strong lang="ja">にゃ / にゅ / にょ</strong> — nya / nyu / nyo
- <strong lang="ja">ひゃ / ひゅ / ひょ</strong> — hya / hyu / hyo
- <strong lang="ja">みゃ / みゅ / みょ</strong> — mya / myu / myo
- <strong lang="ja">りゃ / りゅ / りょ</strong> — rya / ryu / ryo
- **Voiced and semi-voiced forms:** <span lang="ja">ぎゃ, じゃ, びゃ, ぴゃ</span>, and their corresponding <span lang="ja">ゅ</span> and <span lang="ja">ょ</span> combinations.

Remember that the size of the second kana matters. Compare <span lang="ja">きゃ</span> (<em>kya</em>) with <span lang="ja">きや</span> (<em>kiya</em>). The small <span lang="ja">ゃ</span> combines with the preceding kana, while the regular-sized <span lang="ja">や</span> is read separately.

## 2. Sokuon (<span lang="ja">促音</span>) — The Small <span lang="ja">っ</span>

Sokuon is represented by the small <span lang="ja">っ</span>, often called small tsu. It isn't pronounced as a separate <em>tsu</em> sound. Instead, it marks a doubled or geminated consonant that follows it.

When reading aloud, hold the timing briefly before pronouncing the following consonant. This timing matters: <span lang="ja">きて</span> (<em>kite</em>) and <span lang="ja">きって</span> (<em>kitte</em>) are different words.

- <strong lang="ja">きって</strong> — <em>kitte</em> (stamp)
- <strong lang="ja">がっこう</strong> — <em>gakkou</em> (school)
- <strong lang="ja">ちょっと</strong> — <em>chotto</em> (a little)
- <strong lang="ja">きっぷ</strong> — <em>kippu</em> (ticket)

Two important details: small <span lang="ja">っ</span> is written smaller than <span lang="ja">つ</span>, and it generally does not appear at the beginning of native Japanese words. Pay attention to the consonant that follows it, since that determines how the word is read.

## 3. Chouon (<span lang="ja">長音</span>) — Long Vowels

Chouon means a long vowel. In Japanese, vowel length can change a word's meaning, so it is important to hear and pronounce the full duration. A long vowel generally occupies two morae, rather than one.

In Hiragana, long vowels are commonly written by adding another vowel kana. The spelling pattern depends on the vowel and the word.

- <strong lang="ja">おかあさん</strong> — <em>okaasan</em> (mother): the <span lang="ja">あ</span> extends the preceding <em>a</em> sound.
- <strong lang="ja">おにいさん</strong> — <em>oniisan</em> (older brother): the <span lang="ja">い</span> extends the preceding <em>i</em> sound.
- <strong lang="ja">おとうさん</strong> — <em>otousan</em> (father): <span lang="ja">とう</span> has a long <em>o</em> sound.
- <strong lang="ja">おおきい</strong> — <em>ookii</em> (big): <span lang="ja">おお</span> has a long <em>o</em> sound.
- <strong lang="ja">せんせい</strong> — <em>sensei</em> (teacher): <span lang="ja">せい</span> is commonly pronounced with a long <em>e</em> sound.
- <strong lang="ja">がくせい</strong> — <em>gakusei</em> (student): the final <span lang="ja">せい</span> is commonly pronounced with a long <em>e</em> sound.

Notice that <span lang="ja">おう</span> and <span lang="ja">おお</span> can both represent a long <em>o</em> sound. Similarly, <span lang="ja">えい</span> often represents a long <em>e</em> sound. These are useful patterns, but you should learn the spelling of each word rather than assuming every word follows one universal rule.

For now, focus on Hiragana. Katakana has its own common long-vowel spelling convention, which we'll cover when we study Katakana.

## 4. Hatsuon (<span lang="ja">撥音</span>) — The <span lang="ja">ん</span> Sound

Hatsuon refers to the nasal sound represented by <span lang="ja">ん</span>. You have already learned this character, but its pronunciation can vary depending on the surrounding sounds.

- <strong lang="ja">しんぶん</strong> — <em>shinbun</em> (newspaper): before <em>b</em>, the nasal may sound closer to <em>m</em>.
- <strong lang="ja">かんたん</strong> — <em>kantan</em> (simple): notice the nasal before the <em>t</em> sound.
- <strong lang="ja">せんせい</strong> — <em>sensei</em> (teacher): notice the nasal before the <em>s</em> sound.
- <strong lang="ja">ほんや</strong> — <em>hon'ya</em> (bookstore): the apostrophe in romaji makes it clear that the <em>n</em> is separate from the following <em>ya</em>.

You don't need to memorize every phonetic variation yet. For now, learn to recognize <span lang="ja">ん</span>, pronounce it naturally in context, and distinguish it from the following kana when necessary.

<span lang="ja">ん</span> generally does not begin native Japanese words. Its pronunciation can change slightly depending on what comes next, but it remains the same written kana.

### Suggested 5-Day Learning Plan

- **Day 1: Learn Youon.** Practice reading small <span lang="ja">ゃ, ゅ, ょ</span> combinations and distinguishing them from regular-sized kana. Read examples in random order.
- **Day 2: Learn Sokuon.** Compare words with and without small <span lang="ja">っ</span>. Practice the timing of the doubled consonant by reading words aloud.
- **Day 3: Learn Chouon.** Read words with long vowels and practice keeping the vowel long enough. Compare spelling and pronunciation rather than relying only on romaji.
- **Day 4: Learn Hatsuon and Review.** Practice words containing <span lang="ja">ん</span>, then review all four concepts together with the characters from M1 and M2.
- **Day 5: Test Your Progress.** Take a cumulative quiz covering all three milestones. Also test reading aloud and writing separately, since a multiple-choice score cannot measure every skill.

This five-day schedule is a suggested pace, not a deadline. Some concepts may take longer than others, especially when you're learning to hear the difference between short and long sounds. Spend extra time on the areas that still cause confusion.

### Criteria for Completing This Milestone

- **Read Youon Correctly:** Distinguish combinations such as <span lang="ja">きゃ</span> from <span lang="ja">きや</span> and read common youon combinations accurately.
- **Recognize Sokuon:** Identify small <span lang="ja">っ</span> and pronounce words with doubled consonants using the correct timing.
- **Maintain Long Vowels:** Read common long-vowel patterns without shortening the vowel, and recognize that vowel length matters in Japanese pronunciation.
- **Read Hatsuon in Context:** Read words containing <span lang="ja">ん</span> and distinguish it from the kana that follows.
- **Read Mixed Hiragana:** Read familiar words and short, beginner-friendly passages containing material from M1, M2, and M3 without repeatedly stopping to recall each kana's sound.
- **Pass the Cumulative Quiz:** Answer at least 25 out of 30 questions correctly. Review incorrect answers and retest weak areas.

Reading fluency does not mean reading without any pauses. The goal is to stop hesitating over individual kana and to read familiar combinations more naturally. Reading accurately also does not automatically mean understanding every word or sentence.

**One important note:** Completing these three milestones means you've studied the main Hiragana forms and several important reading patterns. It does not mean you've mastered all Japanese reading. Continue practicing with vocabulary, sentences, and beginner-friendly passages to build speed and comprehension over time.

### Quick Self-Test

Try reading these words without looking at the romaji. Check whether you can identify the special sound patterns in each word.

- <strong lang="ja">きょう</strong> — <em>kyou</em> (today): youon and long vowel
- <strong lang="ja">がっこう</strong> — <em>gakkou</em> (school): sokuon and long vowel
- <strong lang="ja">せんせい</strong> — <em>sensei</em> (teacher): hatsuon and long vowel
- <strong lang="ja">しんぶん</strong> — <em>shinbun</em> (newspaper): hatsuon
- <strong lang="ja">おかあさん</strong> — <em>okaasan</em> (mother): long vowel

These five words are a quick check, not a complete mastery test. If you can read them accurately, move on to unfamiliar words and mixed practice to see whether you can apply the rules beyond memorized examples.
